## The one idea

Selection acts on **replicators**, not on organisms — and the replicator is the gene. Your body is a vehicle it builds and discards; you last a lifetime, the gene lasts millions of years. Once you take that perspective, a mass of behaviour that made no sense at the organism level snaps into focus, because the question changes from "why would this animal do that?" to "which gene benefits from a body that does that?"

"Selfish" is the book's great unforced error. It describes genes as behaving *as if* pursuing copies of themselves — a metaphor about mathematics, not a claim about the character of people. Dawkins spent decades correcting the misreading, and the misreading won anyway.

## The engine

**Altruism is arithmetic.** Apparent self-sacrifice is gene-level accounting. Kin selection — Hamilton's rule — says help a relative when the benefit to them, discounted by relatedness, exceeds your cost. You share half your genes with a sibling, an eighth with a cousin; the willingness to die for two siblings or eight cousins is a joke with a real equation behind it. Reciprocal altruism extends the same logic to non-relatives when interactions repeat.

**Evolutionarily stable strategies.** Dawkins imports Maynard Smith's game theory to show how equilibria arise with no planner and no group intention. A population of hawks is invadable by doves and vice versa; the stable point is a mix. This is the deepest tool in the book, and it generalizes far outside biology: stable outcomes emerge from payoffs alone, which is why "everyone would be better off if..." arguments almost never work.

**Memes.** The last chapter, tossed off, and the most culturally consequential thing in it. If a replicator is anything that copies itself with variation and differential survival, genes are only the instance we noticed first. Ideas replicate too, and they are selected for their ability to spread — not for being true, useful, or good for their hosts.

## My take

The clearest science writing I've read, and the rare popular book that gives you a genuinely new instrument rather than a set of facts. The gene's-eye view is a *lens*: it doesn't tell you more about biology so much as let you see the same biology from a position where the puzzles dissolve.

The meme chapter is what I keep returning to, and it has aged into something Dawkins couldn't have intended — it's now the most useful available model for how information behaves in a recommendation-driven world. An idea's fitness and its truth are independent variables, and the modern environment optimizes hard for the first.

## Where it gets thin

The metaphor genuinely misleads, and "it's only a metaphor" is a weaker defence than Dawkins allows — a book's title does work that its footnotes cannot undo. Two generations read it as licence for a cynical account of human motivation that the biology does not support.

Memetics also never became a science. It's a superb metaphor that resisted every attempt to give it units, measurement, or predictions, and it remains a way of *describing* cultural spread rather than explaining it. And the field has moved: multilevel selection is a live position again, epigenetics complicated the clean gene/vehicle split, and the strict gene's-eye view is now one defensible reading rather than the settled one.

## The distilled principle

> Ask what is being copied, and what makes a copy more likely — the answer is rarely what benefits the thing doing the copying.
